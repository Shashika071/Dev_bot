# Research for an R_100 near-barrier Touch bot

Researched 17–18 October 2026. Target: R_100, ONETOUCH, 540 seconds, one selected upper or lower barrier at **0.01 relative price points** from the accepted entry reference. A desired operating range of 10–15 signals per day is a coverage objective. This document does not establish a profitable strategy or a guaranteed daily signal count.

The sources below are original research papers, author/university copies of those papers, or Deriv's own documentation. Results from equities, image classification, and general tabular benchmarks are identified as such. None of the empirical papers reviewed establishes an advantage for this exact R_100 contract.

## Twelve findings and what they mean here

### 1. Instrument mechanics should determine the research target

Deriv describes Synthetic Indices as generated by a cryptographically secure random number generator and unaffected by external news. Its product FAQ says these instruments do not have an order book and cautions against interpreting apparent historical patterns as a dependable technical advantage. These are the operator's descriptions, not an independently reproduced specification of every transition law. **Implication:** omit order-flow, institutional-liquidity, news and session-open explanations unless the actual feed supplies relevant measurements. Test price-derived features empirically; neither an attractive chart nor the RNG description alone proves a feature's usefulness or uselessness. [Deriv, Synthetic Indices, overview and technical-indicator FAQ](https://deriv.com/markets/derived-indices/synthetic-indices?tab=cfds).

An official September 2026 article describes ordinary Volatility Indices as having a fixed volatility target, no built-in drift and two-second ticks, distinguishing one-second variants. This supports checking the observed R_100 cadence and using a driftless null model. Its indicator discussion is educational commentary without a reproducible predictive study, so claims about particular indicators are not validation evidence. [Deriv Dealing Products Team Lead, 24 September 2026, instrument description](https://experts.deriv.com/insights/the-indicators-that-dont-work-on-derived-indices).

### 2. Touch is a path event, and high win rate is different from attractive pricing

Deriv defines Touch by whether the barrier is reached at any time during the contract. Closing direction is therefore the wrong training label. An upper barrier and lower barrier can both be reached during one 540-second path; their probabilities need not sum to one. That last statement follows directly from the event definitions. [Deriv, Touch/No Touch contract description](https://www2.deriv.me/trade-types/options/digital-options/touch-no-touch/).

For a proposal with purchase cost A, total payout P and success probability p, the arithmetic is `EV = p * P - A` and `break_even = A / P`. For example, a hypothetical 2% net winning return needs a 98.039% win probability before costs; 97% success would lose 1.06% of stake on average. These are calculations, not claims about current R_100 quotes. Deriv documents ask price and payout fields, and warns that some proposal fields are optional or may arrive as numeric strings. **Implication:** record and validate the exact executable quote, not a fixed assumed payout. [Deriv API proposal migration documentation](https://developers.deriv.com/comparison/proposal/).

### 3. A discrete first-passage baseline explains frequent wins without any predictive signal

For a random walk starting at zero with continuous, symmetric, independent and identically distributed increments and no drift, the probability of staying strictly below zero for n future observations is

`q_n = choose(2n, n) / 4^n ~ 1 / sqrt(pi*n)`.

This is a consequence of the Sparre Andersen persistence result. A primary research treatment defines survival in equation (17), derives its generating function in equations (61)–(64), and states the central-binomial expression in equation (3). [Majumdar, Schehr & Wergen (2012), pp. 3, 7, 15–16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.6972); [Sparre Andersen (1953), original paper record](https://journals.msp.org/mscand/article/view/2938).

At 270 observations, `q_n = 0.0343196`: crossing the starting level on one specified side has probability approximately **96.568%** under these assumptions. This is a limiting zero-distance illustration, not an estimate for a finite 0.01 barrier. Rounded quotes, zero increments, unequal timing, dependence, drift and entry conventions change applicability. **Implication:** implement an empirical discrete-path null model with the actual finite barrier and quote precision. A neural model reporting about 97% could merely reproduce an ordinary hit frequency.

### 4. Continuous Brownian approximations are particularly delicate at a nearby barrier

Broadie, Glasserman & Kou show that discrete and continuously monitored barrier prices differ materially and derive a correction involving the monitoring interval and volatility. Their numerical discussion explicitly reports poorer approximation accuracy when the barrier is very close to the initial underlying price. [Broadie, Glasserman & Kou (1997), abstract, section 2 and Tables 2.1–2.4](https://www.columbia.edu/~sk75/mfBGK.pdf).

**Implication:** a continuous-volatility hit formula can be a useful diagnostic feature. It should not supply ground-truth labels or an unquestioned 99.9% confidence at a 0.01 offset. Prefer tick-level path replay and discrete simulation/recursion fitted to observed increments. This is an application of the paper's monitoring distinction, not a claim that its risk-neutral option-pricing model is R_100's generator.

### 5. Research supports making pattern definitions objective, rather than trusting a pattern name

Lo, Mamaysky & Wang automate chart-pattern recognition with nonparametric methods and find incremental information in some conditional daily-return distributions for US stocks over 1962–1996. They do not demonstrate a nine-minute Synthetic Index Touch strategy. [Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), abstract and empirical implementation](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/19268/Lo-Mamaysky_wang_foundations.pdf).

**Implication:** if researching candle shapes or breakouts, encode them as reproducible numbers and ask whether they improve a time-separated baseline. A result about conditional stock returns does not establish economically useful barrier-hit predictions at Deriv's quotes. Use body/range, wick/range, close position and distance to a prior range edge before adding a library of named chart patterns.

### 6. Candlestick evidence is mixed across markets; it does not select a winning R_100 candle rule

Marshall, Young & Rose find no value for the tested candlestick strategies on DJIA component stocks over 1992–2002 using an OHLC bootstrap and robustness checks. [Marshall, Young & Rose (2006), abstract, results and conclusion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378426605002116).

Conversely, a 2012 study reports profitability for three bullish two-day reversal patterns in Taiwan stocks, including robustness and out-of-sample exercises. That strategy holds until opposing patterns and uses daily data from 2002–2008. [Lu, Shiu & Liu (2012), abstract and research design](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/bitstream/140.119/61660/1/6368.pdf).

**Implication:** neither blanket dismissal nor automatic trust in candle confluence follows. Closed-bar geometry is a reasonable small challenger feature group; bullish engulfing, doji, hammer or multi-timeframe agreement is not a proven mandatory gate for this contract. Require incremental value on the R_100 target after all feature choices are counted.

### 7. Searching many technical rules changes the evidential standard

Sullivan, Timmermann & White assess technical trading rules using a bootstrap adjustment over the entire rule universe, rather than testing only the winning rule. [Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), abstract and methodology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/0022-1082.00163).

Harvey, Liu & Zhu develop a multiple-testing framework for the cross-section of expected stock returns; their much higher significance hurdle is specific to that factor-search setting. It is not a ready-made threshold for R_100. [Harvey, Liu & Zhu, NBER working paper (2014), published in RFS (2016)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592).

**Implication:** record every candle definition, lookback, direction-selection rule and threshold tried. Use a small preregistered candidate set, time-separated comparisons and appropriate dependence-aware resampling. Do not label a rule validated because its best of hundreds of variants reaches an ordinary unadjusted significance level.

### 8. Repeated strategy selection can overfit a backtest even with an apparent holdout

Bailey and coauthors formalize probability of backtest overfitting and show why selecting among many strategies affects out-of-sample reliability. The linked revised paper is dated 2015. [Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2015 revision), abstract and sections 1–2](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf).

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio addresses selection bias under multiple tests and non-normal return distributions. [Bailey & López de Prado (2014), abstract and selection-bias discussion](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf).

**Implication:** preserve a dated experiment ledger, untouched final periods and a frozen forward trial. Changing parameters repeatedly after viewing the final test consumes that test. PBO/DSR are methodological tools, not automatic certification; a short, dependent series of small binary-option profits and rare full losses needs direct payout-aware uncertainty analysis too.

### 9. Trees and probability calibration deserve a serious comparison with neural models

Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux compare 45 tabular datasets and find tree methods strong on medium-size structured datasets. This supports including shallow boosted trees when observations are rows of engineered features; it does not prove trees always win, or establish an advantage over an LSTM on R_100 tick sequences. [Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux (NeurIPS 2022), benchmark and abstract](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/0378c7692da36807bdec87ab043cdadc-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html).

**Implication:** the first model tournament should include frequency, a discrete hit model, regularized logistic regression and one restrained boosted-tree family. A GAM/spline logistic challenger can express smooth nonlinearities with inspectable effects; including it is an engineering choice, not a claim of proven R_100 superiority. Defer large LSTMs/Transformers and model stacking until these baselines show reproducible limitations.

### 10. Calibration is learned from data and can itself overfit

Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana compare probability prediction and calibration methods. In their benchmarks, less constrained isotonic calibration is more prone to overfitting with small calibration sets; sigmoid calibration often fares better at small sizes. Their observed sample-size ranges are benchmark findings, not universal minimums for a rare-failure Touch target. [Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana (ICML 2005), section 5 and Figure 7](https://icml.cc/Conferences/2005/proceedings/papers/079_GoodProbabilities_NiculescuMizilCaruana.pdf).

**Implication:** calibrate every candidate on an earlier dedicated period, choose models/thresholds on a later selection period, and evaluate once on a final period. Count misses and effective independent windows, not just ticks. Compare calibration-frequency shrinkage, sigmoid and identity only as justified by data; do not manufacture confidence through a flexible calibrator fitted to a handful of failures.

### 11. Probability quality and economic decision quality both need to be measured

Gneiting & Raftery establish the rationale for proper scoring rules, including quadratic/Brier and logarithmic scores: honest probabilistic forecasts optimize expected score. Such scores assess forecast quality, not profitability under arbitrary payouts. [Gneiting & Raftery (2007), introduction and categorical scoring rules](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf).

**Implication:** report calibrated Brier/log loss and reliability against frequency and discrete-path baselines. Separately report quote-adjusted net return, loss frequency, drawdown and uncertainty for the selected trades. AUC can help inspect ranking, but does not establish accuracy of probabilities near an executable break-even threshold. The two evaluations answer different questions.

### 12. Abstention is a design feature; signal coverage is an outcome to measure

Selective-classification research formalizes exchanging coverage for lower error by rejecting uncertain examples. Its reported guarantees are under its own statistical conditions and image-classification experiments, not a guarantee for dependent R_100 outcomes or option returns. [Geifman & El-Yaniv (NeurIPS 2017), abstract and selective risk/coverage definitions](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/4a8423d5e91fda00bb7e46540e2b0cf1-Paper.pdf).

**Implication:** plot held-out net performance, calibration and number of signals across predeclared thresholds, then freeze a policy before the final test. Optimize coverage subject to evidence and risk constraints; avoid lowering the threshold mid-day to fill a quota. A 10–15/day target can be feasible operationally while remaining empirically unsupported as a profitable coverage level.

## Ranked research candidates for this particular target

This ranking is a proposed order of experiments, based on target mechanics and simplicity. It is not a ranking of demonstrated R_100 profitability.

| Priority | Family | Minimal implementation | What would justify keeping it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empirical frequency | Separate upper/lower 540-second hit frequencies with shrinkage, computed from training-only precision-correct complete windows. Include an unconditional and a modest rolling challenger. | Establishes whether any complex model beats a valid probability baseline. |
| 2 | Discrete hit/survival | Simulate or recursively propagate observed training-only tick increments to the finite ±0.01 barrier, respecting quantization, observed cadence and accepted entry timing. Compare an iid null with a limited dependence-preserving challenger if dependence is measured. | Stable calibration and hit-frequency predictions; checks whether near-barrier performance is simply first-passage mechanics. |
| 3 | Directional momentum/mean reversion | Regularized logistic model, then optionally a small spline/GAM model. Use side-normalized return sums, short/long volatility, lagged sign balance and normalized deviation from a recent mean. Shrink drift toward zero. | Incremental calibrated out-of-period skill versus 1–2, plus positive quoted economics. No presumed trend/reversion effect. |
| 4 | Shallow boosted trees | One small XGBoost or CatBoost candidate with restrained depth, regularization and a small fixed search budget. Use exactly the same feature snapshots as 3. | Repeated improvement over simple models; no complexity preference when tied. |
| 5 | Candle geometry | Ablation adding a few closed 1-minute/5-minute body, wick, close-location and range ratios. Express distances in volatility/step units as appropriate. | Demonstrated incremental value beyond returns/volatility. Do not double-count named patterns derived from the same OHLC values. |
| 6 | Breakout/range position | Distances to past completed 1/5/15-minute extrema, prior range width and whether a completed bar crossed a prior bound. | Survives a separate ablation. No inference about absent order-book liquidity. |
| 7 | Regime conditioning | Short/long variance ratio, gap/tie rates, tails, sign autocorrelation and fit stability. Primarily a data-quality/shift diagnostic; conditional forecasts only if useful. | Genuine reproducible heterogeneity. A constant target volatility does not justify inventing session/news regimes. |
| 8 | Neural sequences / survival networks | A small sequence challenger only after the simpler tournament is stable; preserve identical endpoint, target and evaluation periods. Multi-horizon survival only if time-to-hit becomes a real product need. | Enough effective data and forward evidence of a material improvement after computation and selection costs. No reviewed primary evidence establishes this for the requested contract. |

## Minimal concrete build and evaluation plan

1. **Define an exact contract record.** Store symbol, signed finite price-point offset, duration, quote spot/reference, precision, entry delay, observation times and settlement convention. Validate ±0.01/540-second proposals for the intended account/context. Never silently substitute a different barrier or contract. Train upper and lower events separately; replay the final direction-selection policy jointly so the benefit of choosing between them is honestly evaluated.
2. **Build a clean chronological dataset.** Preserve tick precision and gaps; do not interpolate a barrier hit through missing observations. Use broker outcomes to audit the replay labels. Features must use information available at decision time, and candle features must use completed candles. Purge training labels that extend into calibration/selection/test periods; account for the 540-second horizon plus entry latency. Overlapping observations are dependent even if stored as separate rows.
3. **Run only the first four model families initially.** Freeze a compact feature set and search budget. Treat candle geometry, breakout distances and regime variables as three separately logged ablations, rather than combining every indicator and several hard veto gates at once. Calibrate candidates fairly and allow a frequency/simple model to win.
4. **Separate prediction from trade selection.** Select the side using conservative quote-adjusted evidence, allow no-trade, and log rejected decisions with reason codes. Compare side probabilities with exact contemporaneous break-even values. Refresh the proposal and enforce the final execution checks if an executable order is ever requested; a historically positive EV calculation on another quote is insufficient.
5. **Measure coverage without forcing it.** Replay caps/cooldowns and actual quote availability. Report daily signal distribution (including zero-signal days), dependence, calibration, net return intervals and largest loss sequences. The desired 10–15/day range is a threshold-selection objective on development data, not a promised minimum. A maximum cap and a daily minimum are different controls.
6. **Freeze a forward shadow/demo trial.** Save model/data hashes and thresholds, log accepted broker entry/settlement and every decision, and compare the frozen challenger with baselines. Changes create a new experiment with a new evaluation period. A model does not become better supported merely because additional patterns were added.

## Limits that should be visible to the owner

There is no honest basis in the reviewed literature to guarantee a high winning percentage, profit, or 10–15 qualifying trades every day for this exact contract. A close barrier can create frequent wins without forecasting skill, and the quoted payout can absorb that advantage. This is a testable problem with useful baselines, rather than a reason to assume all price features are either valuable or worthless.

Sample size must be judged by failures and dependence. Under an independent constant-rate binomial model, 100 wins and no losses still gives a one-sided 95% upper failure probability of `1 - 0.05^(1/100)`, about 2.95%. That arithmetic does not establish 99.9% reliability. Dependence and trying many policies weaken conclusions further. No fixed number of ticks, candles, or days alone certifies the strategy.

The most defensible initial improvement is an exact-contract, discrete-path probability tournament with a conservative quote-aware abstention policy and an honest readiness/performance display. Which candidate is best remains an empirical result to establish on this target.
